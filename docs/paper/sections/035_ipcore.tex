\section{The core as an intellectual-property block}
\label{sec:ipcore}

The sections that follow describe the internals of the core. This section describes it from the outside, the way an intellectual-property (IP) block is described to an integrator: its ports, its configuration space, the ways it is integrated into a larger system, and the verification collateral delivered with it. Everything here is realised in the artifact; the section collects it so that the title's claim can be checked.

\subsection{Ports}
\label{sec:ipcore-ports}

Table~\ref{tab:ports} lists the interfaces of the core. Three of them are fixed by the statement of Section~\ref{sec:statement}: a program image, an input stream and a hint stream go in, and a proof and its public values come out. The remaining interfaces are the ones an integrator connects: a verifying key derived once per program, a verifier in one of two forms, an extension interface for accelerators, and the key allowlist that binds a recursion proof to the advertised machine.

\subsection{Realisation platform}
\label{sec:ipcore-platform}

A silicon IP core is delivered as a hardware description and characterised on a target process; the same core yields a different frequency, area and power on each node, and the deliverable itself is process-independent. This core is delivered as a constraint system---the tables, their polynomial identities and their bus interactions---which plays the role of the description, and it is realised by a prover that evaluates and commits those tables on a given accelerator, which plays the role of the process. Two consequences run through the rest of the paper. \emph{Area is a property of the core}: the trace cells of Section~\ref{sec:performance} are counted from the constraint system and the execution, and are the same on any machine. Trace area is the counterpart of die area, and it plays the same economic role. Die area sets what a wafer yields and therefore the unit cost of a part; trace area sets how many field elements the prover must commit and open, and therefore the cost of a proof. It is not measured in square millimetres and does not translate into one: a unit's width is its column count and its activity is its row count, so the same design has the same area on every accelerator, while the time that area costs is set by the accelerator (below). Halving trace area is the counterpart of a shrink, and Section~\ref{sec:performance} is the corresponding datasheet. \emph{Throughput is a property of the pair}: the megahertz of proved execution are what one particular accelerator, an NVIDIA RTX~5090, achieves on this core, and a different accelerator or a different number of them moves the figure without changing the design, which is why Section~\ref{sec:performance} reports scaling from one to four devices and keeps measurements on other projects' hardware separate. The relation between the two is the cost model of \S\ref{sec:perf-profile}: area and interaction count predict prover time, and the accelerator sets the constant.

\subsection{Configuration space}
\label{sec:ipcore-config}

The core is parameterised, and the evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:performance} is a walk through part of its configuration space. The parameters are: the shard size, which trades the per-shard setup and the recursion leaf against the memory of the lookup circuit's first layer (Section~\ref{sec:performance} measures four values); the stacking height of the commitment, which sets the stripe count; the \WHIR{} schedule---the number of committed rounds, their folding factors and code rates, the query counts, the out-of-domain samples and the query grinding---which sets the proof size and the soundness accounting (Section~\ref{sec:iopp}); the grinding on the lookup challenges; the recursion arity; and the accelerator set, which determines the unit set of the precompile shards and hence the verifying keys. The values of a given release are in its configuration file. Changing any parameter that enters a constraint system changes the verifying keys of the affected units and therefore the recursion-key allowlist, which is regenerated once per release; parameters that only enter the schedule (query counts, grinding) change the keys of the recursion programs but not of the core units.

\subsection{Integration paths}
\label{sec:ipcore-integration}

The integrations below share the same ports. The first is the general shape, the second and third are deployed today, and the last two are enabled by the same interface but not yet deployed. (0) \emph{Hash-intensive verifiable computation.} The common shape is a guest that hashes: it recomputes a Merkle root over data the verifier does not hold, checks an inclusion or update proof, verifies a chain of signatures or certificates, or attests a firmware image or a log. The guest is compiled by an ordinary MIPS toolchain, calls the hash accelerators of Section~\ref{sec:isa} where the volume justifies it, and the verifier checks one proof whose cost does not grow with the amount hashed. (1) \emph{Block proving.} A coordinating process executes the guest natively, cuts the run into shards and ships a chunk descriptor per shard to worker processes, one per GPU, which re-execute and prove their shards and the recursion nodes; the compressed proof is published to the block-proving dashboard~\citep{ethproofs}. Section~\ref{sec:perf-host} describes the host budget under which this pipeline was engineered. (2) \emph{On-chain verification.} The compressed proof is wrapped to Groth16 over BN254 and checked by a standard on-chain verifier; the wrap is the only stage with a trusted setup. (3) \emph{Hybrid fault and validity proving.} Because the core executes the same instruction set as Cannon and Kona~\citep{cannon,kona}, one program image can be adjudicated by a fault proof and certified by a validity proof, without a second toolchain or a second build. (4) \emph{Embedded and IoT workloads.} A program compiled by a device's own MIPS toolchain is executed by a remote prover, and a gateway or a phone checks the proof; the statement is the execution of the device's software rather than a property of the device (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\subsection{Verification collateral}
\label{sec:ipcore-collateral}

The block is delivered with the collateral of Section~\ref{sec:verification}: the specification-derived conformance suite and the Cannon suite with their reference results; the executable \Lean{} model of the ISA and its two checks against the emulator; one extracted determinism theorem per unit and opcode selector, with the tactic that discharges them and the list of open ones; the soundness configuration and the generated accounting report for the deployed schedule; and the defects register (Table~\ref{tab:defects}). An integrator therefore verifies once that the toolchain produces the image it expects and that the verifier binary and the key allowlist are the delivered ones. The correspondence between the constraint system and the ISA, and the uniqueness of the constraint system's behaviour, are addressed by the collateral to the extent Section~\ref{sec:verification} records---the first by testing against an independent reference and a formal model, the second by \ndetproved{} of \ndettotal{} closed unit theorems and a uniqueness theorem that is stated rather than machine-checked---and do not have to be re-established per integration.
