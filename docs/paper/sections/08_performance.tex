\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance}

We evaluate three quantities: \emph{instruction efficiency}, the committed area and bus interactions an executed instruction costs, which is a property of the arithmetisation (\S\ref{sec:perf-estimate}); \emph{proving efficiency}, the rate at which an implementation turns that area into a proof on named hardware (\S\ref{sec:perf-prover}); and \emph{proof size}, the bytes of the compressed proof a verifier reads, with the parameters that control it (\S\ref{sec:perf-proofsize}). One primary workload, one GPU model and one host are used, with interleaved repetitions, so that differences between configurations are attributable. No cross-system comparison is attempted: other systems' figures are self-reported on different guests and mostly on clusters, and nothing here is a same-hardware baseline.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:perf-setup}

\paragraph{Workload.} Ethereum mainnet block 25\,907\,955, executed by a MIPS32 build of the Reth execution client with an arena-represented witness trie; the guest hashes trie nodes, recomputes a Merkle root over state it does not hold and verifies signatures. A word-wise \texttt{memcmp}, a zero-copy Keccak wrapper and the arena trie reduce it from 379 to 288 million cycles. The single-GPU timing of \emph{Memory traffic and shard size} (\S\ref{sec:perf-after}) uses that cached record; the multi-GPU measurements of Table~\ref{tab:cards} use three blocks of Table~\ref{tab:clients} with the Reth guest, 26\,138\,428, 26\,138\,416 and 26\,138\,421 (206, 348 and 508\,M cycles), and the instruction-efficiency census of \S\ref{sec:perf-profile} uses block 25\,955\,640 (495.6\,M cycles) in the analysed configuration.

\paragraph{Hardware.} One NVIDIA RTX~5090 (32\,GB) in a virtual machine with eight such GPUs, 124 vCPUs of AMD EPYC 9355 and 925\,GB of memory; the runs of Table~\ref{tab:cards} use one, two and four, and those of Table~\ref{tab:clients} one and all eight (driver 570.153.02, CUDA 12.8, Ubuntu 22.04). The GPU backend is a separate CUDA implementation of the same protocol and is not open source.

\paragraph{Configurations.}\label{sec:perf-revision} Sections~\ref{sec:air}--\ref{sec:security} describe the \emph{analysed configuration}. Most timings below, and the proof-size comparison of \S\ref{sec:perf-after}, were taken on an earlier \emph{measured configuration}, which differs in five respects: the $\Poseidon{}$ permutation used 13 partial rounds rather than the corrected 20; the lookup challenge was not ground; the syscall bus carried the sum of two 16-bit half-words rather than the half-words themselves (\S\ref{sec:air-chips}); the set of three soundness fixes of Table~\ref{tab:det-findings} was not applied; and the system-call tables did not enumerate the numbers they accept (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-isa}). Each of the last two adds constraints. Comparisons between measured-configuration runs are therefore internal to that configuration, and the soundness figures of \S\ref{sec:iopp-soundness} are for the analysed one. Unless a caption or the text says otherwise, every timing below is of the measured configuration and every area of the analysed one; the exceptions are the timings of Tables~\ref{tab:cards} and~\ref{tab:clients} and the compressed-proof size of \S\ref{sec:perf-proofsize}, which are of the analysed configuration.

\paragraph{Metrics and protocol.} \emph{Trace area} (\S\ref{sec:prelim-chips}) is taken from the emulator's shard-close census; Table~\ref{tab:census} takes its kernel shares from the measured configuration. \emph{Proving time}, used by Tables~\ref{tab:cards} and~\ref{tab:clients}, runs from the prove request at the prover to the compressed proof, over shard proving and recursion, and excludes the client's execution and verification; the measured-configuration timings are \emph{wall-clock}, from reading the cached input to writing the compressed proof, and include 2--3\,s of host verification. \emph{Proving throughput} is guest cycles divided by the time, and is not a clock frequency. \emph{Proof size} is the serialised compressed proof. A ten-shard \texttt{nsys} sample attributes kernel time. Table~\ref{tab:cards} reports the median of three consecutive warm proofs per configuration, warm meaning after a first proof has loaded the keys and compiled the programs; the three were within 2.0\,s of each other. No other proving ran on the host during the runs of Tables~\ref{tab:cards} and~\ref{tab:clients}.

\paragraph{Per instruction.} The frame is most of every common row: an addition or bitwise operation pays 29--32 cells and 16--20 interactions of frame for at most five cells and four interactions of its own, so the per-instruction floor is set by the register memory check. Expensive instructions are expensive in columns, not rows: a division is one row of 163 cells where Jolt's sequence takes eight steps~\citep{jolt}. Per-opcode chips are what keep the common rows narrow: one table carrying every opcode's columns would pay the widest row on every instruction, over 400 cells (\texttt{MiscInstrs} alone has 432 columns) where the common instructions pay 33 to 37; SP1 makes the same choice~\citep{sp1}.

\paragraph{Per block.}\label{sec:perf-profile} Block 25\,955\,640, 495.6 million cycles, commits 29.2\,G cells and raises 12.8\,G (row, interaction) pairs, 58.9 cells and 25.8 pairs per executed instruction (Table~\ref{tab:census}, Figure~\ref{fig:area}). This is larger than the single-row costs of Table~\ref{tab:estimate} mainly because of the per-shard memory-argument rows: the \texttt{Global} chip holds 15\% of the area, 8.8 cells per instruction, because every word a shard touches costs two rows of it, which is the cost of sharding. The figure depends on the workload, not only on the instruction set. A precompile call is one guest cycle and many rows of an accelerator chip, and since a shard closes at the area budget (\S\ref{sec:isa-record}), the precompile share sets both the cells per cycle and the shard count: two Ethereum mainnet blocks of 949 and 943 million cycles proved in production as 702 and 213 shards. These are cells of a 31-bit field and do not convert into Jolt's bit-weighted count.

\paragraph{Effect of reducing area.}\label{sec:perf-changes} The census says where to cut: area for the commitment and constraint argument, pairs for the bus argument. Two changes follow from it, both mechanisms rather than tuning: replacing a boolean sign decomposition in the cross-shard digest chip with three byte lookups, and dropping byte checks on memory-instruction accesses that another chip already enforces. Both change the constraint system, and with it the chip keys and the recursion-key allowlist; neither changes the statement proved.

\paragraph{Time breakdown.} Kernels account for about 340\,ms of the 500\,ms of GPU time per shard in the measured configuration, launch gaps and host synchronisation the rest (Table~\ref{tab:census}, Figure~\ref{fig:kernel}). The lookup argument is the largest consumer, and its cost scales with (row, interaction) pairs rather than with columns: memory-instruction chips carry 25--26 interactions per row and 47\% of all pairs, while the \texttt{Global} chip holds 15\% of the area but 3.7\% of the pairs. The cost model is therefore area for the commitment and zerocheck and pairs for the lookup, one pair weighted as about two cells.

\paragraph{Memory traffic and shard size.}\label{sec:perf-after} A byte-level profile of the \LogUp{} circuit showed that its first layer paired fractions across the most-significant index bit, so short chips were carried through every layer of the tallest and the traffic summed over layers was 5.2$\times$ that of the first. The prover therefore pairs on the least-significant bit, so each chip folds out after $\log_2$ of its own height, and the memory this frees admits shards of twice the size. The two changes are enabled together: the old-pairing, doubled-shard configuration does not fit in memory, so their effects are not separated. With both, the cached 288\,M-cycle block 25\,907\,955 proves in 48.7\,s on one GPU ($5.9$\,MHz), against 67.8\,s for the revision without either at the largest shard size it could run ($-28\%$), with the compressed proof unchanged in size.

\paragraph{Scaling across devices.}\label{sec:perf-scaling} Table~\ref{tab:cards} and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} report proving time and throughput on one to four GPUs for three blocks of Table~\ref{tab:clients} proved with the Reth guest, of 206, 348 and 508\,M cycles. Two GPUs prove each block $1.9\times$ faster than one and four $3.1$--$3.5\times$, reaching 15--23\,MHz. The gap to linear scaling is the serial recursion tail and the start-up interval before the emulator has produced a shard for every GPU; both are fixed per block, so the larger the block, the closer the scaling is to linear.

\paragraph{Execution clients.}\label{sec:perf-eight}\label{sec:perf-clients} \Ziren{} proves Ethereum blocks with more than one execution client compiled to MIPS32 from different languages: Reth, written in Rust, and the block keeper of Geth, written in Go, whose runtime issues the no-op Linux calls of Appendix~\ref{sec:app-isa} while starting. Table~\ref{tab:clients} proves the same 16 consecutive Ethereum mainnet blocks, 26\,138\,415 to 26\,138\,430, with both clients on the same GPUs, first one and then eight. Both guests call the precompiles for Keccak, SHA-256 and the elliptic curves, and the Geth guest was tuned for 32-bit MIPS in its BN254 backend, hashing, jump-destination analysis, signature recovery and trie handling, which removes 43\% of its cycles on these blocks. A shard costs the same GPU time under both guests, 0.48\,s on one GPU in this configuration, so a block costs in proportion to its shards rather than its cycles. The Reth guest also runs the EVM's 256-bit arithmetic on a precompile, so its shards close after 2.3 million cycles on average against 5.1 million for Geth. It therefore runs at the lower rate and still proves every block in less time: $1.8$--$3.9\times$ less on one GPU and $1.3$--$2.6\times$ less on eight. Eight GPUs reduce the summed proving time $5.5\times$ for Geth and $3.9\times$ for Reth; the Reth blocks are smaller, so the serial tail after the last shard (\S\ref{sec:perf-pipeline}) is a larger share of each.

\paragraph{The host.}\label{sec:perf-emu}\label{sec:perf-host} On one core of an AMD EPYC~9355, the just-in-time compiler executes block 25\,955\,640 (496\,M cycles) at 198\,MHz once compiled (166\,MHz including its single compilation pass), and the interpreter at 40.6\,MHz without the event record and 14.6\,MHz with it; the coordinator's whole producer job takes 6.1\,s for a 420\,M-cycle block, some 69\,MHz. On one GPU, emulation does not bind. It binds when one host feeds several GPUs, because each shard is re-emulated by the worker that proves it, at about one second of host time per shard against half a second of GPU time in the measured configuration, so the event record carries only what a column witnesses and the coordinator never holds the whole execution (\S\ref{sec:arch-execution}).

\paragraph{GPU implementation.} Three kinds of engineering act on different costs. \emph{Fused kernels} cut memory traffic: the jagged fold and the round polynomial it feeds are one kernel, as are the layer transition and the round over the mixed round-0 slab. \emph{A cross-stage pipeline} cuts idleness (\S\ref{sec:perf-pipeline}). \emph{Occupancy tuning} caps the register budget for the measured card. Field arithmetic rests on the inline-PTX Montgomery primitives of \texttt{sppark}~\citep{sppark}, which we use and did not write.

\paragraph{Prover cost of the soundness target.}\label{sec:perf-grind} The level of \S\ref{sec:iopp-soundness} is paid for in queries and in proof-of-work. We measured the grinds separately, on four GPUs, at a build that grinds the lookup challenge, with the arms interleaved on one cached block. The $22/22/14$-bit query, \LogUp{} and batching grinds took $33.4$\,s against $33.9$\,s for $16/16/8$, a difference within run-to-run spread, while buying the same bits with queries (133, 95 and 91 queries in the three \WHIR{} rounds at a 16-bit grind) cost $8.6$\%. That holds only because the search runs on the device: with the grinds on the host the same schedule took $166$\,s, $123$\,s of it the \LogUp{} grind.

\subsection{Controlling proof size}
\label{sec:perf-proofsize}

The shard proofs of a block total on the order of $10^{8}$ bytes before recursion; the published object is the compressed proof at the root of the tree, whose size does not grow with the execution. It is dominated by the openings of the first \WHIR{} oracle, about four fifths of it in the instrumented proofs, because each query authenticates one coset row of every stripe; the later \WHIR{} rounds, the \LogUp{} layer proofs and the other sumchecks share the rest. To first order the size is
\[
  t_0 \cdot \big(\text{stripes} \cdot 2^{k_0} + \text{roots} \cdot \log_2(\text{leaves}) \cdot |\text{digest}|\big) + \textstyle\sum_{i \ge 1} t_i \cdot (\text{folded leaf} + \text{path}),
\]
with $t_i$ the queries of round $i$ and $k_0$ the first folding factor. Every lever on it is a parameter of the schedule (\S\ref{sec:iopp}), fixed per stage, and each trades against something else at a fixed soundness level:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Queries per round.} A query at rate $\rho$ yields $-\log_2\frac{1+\rho}{2}$ bits under unique decoding and close to $\frac12\log_2\frac1\rho$ under the Johnson bound, which is also proven but adds proximity-gap terms paid for by the grinding of \S\ref{sec:iopp-commit}. Lowering $\rho$ therefore cuts every round's queries, most of all under the Johnson-bound analysis; the first round's rate is bounded by the field, since the stacking height times $1/\rho$ must not exceed the two-adic subgroup ($2^{24}$ for \KB{}), and the prover's encoding work and memory grow as $1/\rho$.
  \item \emph{Grinding.} Each bit of query grinding removes about one bit's worth of queries, at a doubling of the search, which on the device is cheap (\S\ref{sec:perf-grind}); the size of a grinding witness, one field element, bounds how far this goes.
  \item \emph{Leaf width.} A smaller first folding factor $k_0$ narrows every first-round leaf and moves the variables to later rounds, whose queries are fewer.
  \item \emph{Stripes.} The number of stripes a query opens follows the committed area of the stage, which for a compress node is set by the recursion verifier program rather than by the guest.
\end{itemize}

The parameters that make the proof small need to apply only to the published proof: the core shards and every recursion node below the root keep the schedule tuned for prover time, since each of those proofs is consumed by its parent and never leaves the prover, and the root alone proves under the compress schedule of Table~\ref{tab:whir-schedule}. The root is a compose of arity one over the node that covers the execution (\S\ref{sec:recursion}), so its verifier program, and with it the committed area that sets the number of stripes, is the same small one for every tree, and it fits the smallest area class, 16 stripes per round. The two schedules are two instances of one ring type, so the field, the hash and the transcript are shared and only the schedule differs; a verifier takes the schedule from the stage it expects, never from the proof.

Taken together, the Johnson-bound schedule at a first rate of $2^{-3}$ with $k_0 = 2$ opens $58 \cdot 2^{2}$ values per stripe in the first oracle against $124 \cdot 2^{3}$ for the unique-decoding schedule at rate $2^{-2}$ with $k_0 = 3$, under a quarter, at the same union soundness (Table~\ref{tab:components}), for 27 bits of grinding on the folding and batching challenges, paid once per proof at the root, which is the prover-time cost of the smaller proof. Measured on the analysed configuration with a fibonacci guest, the compressed proof is 280\,653 bytes (274\,KiB) under the compress schedule, against 846\,401 bytes when the root proves under the core schedule; since the root program is the same for every tree, the size does not depend on the guest. The native verifier checks it in 73\,ms (median of five runs, 63--89\,ms) on the host of \S\ref{sec:perf-setup}. This run omits the in-circuit allowlist check of the root's children, since the guest's leaf key lies outside the enumerated clusters (\S\ref{sec:recursion}).

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:perf-threats}

\emph{Internal.} The spread of the interleaved repetitions is small relative to the effects reported, though another process shared the host (\S\ref{sec:perf-setup}). \emph{Construct.} The timings of Table~\ref{tab:census} and of the ablations predate the $\Poseidon{}$ correction, which adds seven partial rounds to commitments, transcripts and recursion and changes absolute performance by an unmeasured amount; they are evidence about mechanisms to the extent the correction leaves relative costs unchanged. The timings of Tables~\ref{tab:cards} and~\ref{tab:clients} and the compressed-proof size postdate it (\S\ref{sec:perf-revision}). Trace area is a model where the kernel profile is the measurement. The pipelining of \S\ref{sec:perf-pipeline} is not ablated: no run defers recursion until every shard is proved, so its effect on wall-clock time is not separated from the rest. \emph{External.} The primary workload is one Ethereum block, and other guests have different instruction mixes. The raw per-run timings are not retained, and the retained area census is for the analysed configuration rather than the measured one.
