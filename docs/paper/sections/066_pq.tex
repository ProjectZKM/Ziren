\subsection{Post-quantum security}
\label{sec:pq}

Every component of the shard and recursion arguments is hash-based: the Merkle commitments under $\Hash$, the \WHIR{} proximity test, the sumchecks and \LogUp{}, and the global challenge of \S\ref{sec:air-global}, a hash chain that seeds one more \LogUp{} challenge. This paper computes no quantum security level and claims post-quantum security for no configuration.

\paragraph{The cited bound.} Chiesa, Manohar and Spooner show that the compilation of Theorem~\ref{thm:bcs}, applied to a public-coin interactive oracle proof with round-by-round soundness error $\varepsilon$, has soundness error $O(t^2\,\varepsilon + t^3/2^{\lambda})$ against a quantum adversary that makes $t$ superposition queries to a random oracle with $\lambda$ output bits, and is an argument of knowledge when the proof has the round-by-round knowledge property~\citep[Thm.~3]{cms19}. We have read the theorem in its informal statement and print no constants.

For one node, under hypothesis~1 and with a random oracle for $\Hash$, the first term stays below one while $t^2 \cdot 2^{-128} < 1$, up to $t = 2^{64}$ queries, and the second while $t^3 < 2^{247.91}$, up to $t = 2^{82.6}$. For the tree there is no theorem. With the union bound of the illustrative tree, $2^{-116.94}$, in the place of $\varepsilon$, as the classical sum over nodes suggests, the first term reaches one at $t = 2^{116.94/2} = 2^{58.5}$. The statistical term binds, not the collision term, and the quadratic loss applies to all of the statistical soundness: the bits that the queries and the field provide as much as the bits of grinding. By this bound, 128 bits in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:level}, with quantum queries as the work, would need per-round errors near $2^{-256}$ and a digest of about 384 bits.

\paragraph{Search costs.} A quantum collision search on a function with $N = 2^{247.91}$ outputs needs $\Theta(N^{1/3}) = 2^{82.6}$ queries, which is optimal for random functions~\citep{bht98,zhandry-collision}, and a table of that size queried in superposition, at a price-performance ratio that Bernstein argues is worse than classical parallel search~\citep{bernstein09}. The best known algorithm with few qubits takes time $\tilde O(2^{2n/5})$ on an $n$-bit digest, $2^{99.2}$ here, with $\tilde O(2^{n/5}) = 2^{49.6}$ of classical memory~\citep{cns17}, and NIST's evaluation criteria give collision search on a 256-bit hash a classical gate count only~\citep{nist-pqc-cfp}. Classical birthday search on this digest costs $2^{124}$. A $b$-bit proof of work costs a quantum prover about $2^{b/2}$ sequential iterations~\citep{grover96}.

\paragraph{What no theorem covers.} (i) The recursion tree: the classical composition theorem has no quantum statement~\citep{cgsy24}. (ii) The hash, a public invertible permutation in three modes and not a random function: the sponge is proved quantum indifferentiable with a bound its authors call loose~\citep{acmt25}, and we know no quantum result for a duplex-sponge transcript or for a Merkle tree of truncated permutations. (iii) Grinding: placing $2^{-b/2}$ in a round's error is a modelling step. (iv) Cost: queries are not gates, and no figure above is a running time.

\paragraph{The alternatives.} The elliptic-curve multiset hash that the global challenge replaced was the exception among the components: Shor's algorithm computes discrete logarithms in $E(\mathbb F_{p^7})$~\citep{shor97}, which reduces its soundness to finding a short integer relation among known scalars, to be assumed easy at the interaction counts of a block. Of the other options of \S\ref{sec:air-global}, Merkle roots rest on hashes and a lattice multiset hash on a problem not known to be easy for quantum adversaries. The pairing-based wraps of \S\ref{sec:applications} are not post-quantum; the statement concerns the compressed proof.
